\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{E}{mergency} assessment protocols---including the \acf{ABCDE}
survey and \acf{FAST} ultrasound---enable clinicians to rapidly identify
life-threatening conditions at the point of care. In mass-casualty incidents,
disaster sites, and hazardous environments, however, trained personnel are
often unavailable or overwhelmed, creating a critical bottleneck in triage.
A legged mobile manipulator that can autonomously navigate unstructured
terrain, locate a patient, and perform these assessments could extend
emergency care to scenarios where human responders cannot safely operate.

Autonomous robotic ultrasound has advanced significantly on fixed-base
platforms. Systems employing impedance control, deep-learning-based
segmentation, and pre-planned scan protocols have demonstrated clinically
relevant probe positioning without direct human
guidance~\cite{mathiassen2016, li2021overview, duan2022, farsoni2025autonomous}.
These results, however, share a fundamental limitation: the patient must be
pre-positioned within the arm's reachable workspace, confining autonomous
ultrasound to controlled clinical settings.

Legged platforms such as the Boston Dynamics Spot remove this constraint.
They can traverse stairs, rubble, and confined spaces inaccessible to wheeled
robots, and can therefore reach patients wherever they lie. Mounting a
manipulator arm on a legged base introduces, however, a deeper challenge that
goes beyond motion coordination. The robot does not know the patient's
location a priori, and the quality of its perception depends critically on how
it positions its own body: camera viewpoint, height, and orientation all
affect detection accuracy. Existing \ac{WBC} formulations for legged
manipulators~\cite{bellicoso2019, sleiman2021, haviland2022} simultaneously
optimise base and arm motion, but assume known or static targets and do not
account for the coupling between body posture and perceptual quality. In a
realistic emergency scenario, the robot must actively shape its own perception
before it can act.

This paper presents \acf{TERESA} (Trustworthy Emergency Robot for Efficient
Support and Assistance), an integrated system for autonomous emergency
assessment on a Boston Dynamics Spot legged robot equipped with a Unitree Z1
manipulator arm. Building on the fixed-base scanning competence established
in~\cite{farsoni2025autonomous}, we extend autonomous assessment to a fully
mobile platform capable of operating in unstructured environments. The core
contribution of \ac{TERESA} is a whole-body active perception framework: the
robot continuously adapts its posture---body height, pitch, and yaw---not
merely to minimise kinematic error, but to maximise the quality of its
perceptual input.

The system is built around four principles. First, a \textbf{confidence-gated
active search} phase explores body configurations that improve detection
reliability, committing to the approach only once the patient has been
localised with high confidence. Second, an \textbf{anticipatory body scanner}
acquires multi-view anatomical data during navigation, eliminating the idle
time of traditional stop-then-scan pipelines. Third, a
\textbf{perception-quality-aware velocity controller} scales the approach
speed in proportion to detection confidence, avoiding both premature
commitment to uncertain targets and unnecessary stops. Fourth, a
\textbf{pre-planned body reconfiguration} step optimises the robot's posture
for each anatomical scanning target through an offline search, maximising arm
manipulability without requiring real-time whole-body optimisation.

The main contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{whole-body active perception framework} for legged mobile
        manipulation, in which body posture is optimised to improve
        perceptual quality rather than solely to minimise kinematic error
        (Section~\ref{sec:active_perception}).
  \item A \textbf{confidence-gated search strategy} that decouples patient
        localisation from approach, ensuring robust detection before the
        robot commits to navigation (Section~\ref{sec:active_perception}).
  \item An \textbf{anticipatory multi-view scanning pipeline} that acquires
        patient anatomy concurrently with base motion, removing the idle
        periods inherent to sequential stop-and-scan architectures
        (Section~\ref{sec:system_architecture}).
  \item \textbf{Experimental validation} on a physical Spot+Z1 platform,
        demonstrating fully autonomous emergency assessment with a
        \XX\,\% reduction in idle time compared to a stop-and-manipulate
        baseline (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

To the best of our knowledge, \ac{TERESA} is the first legged robotic system to
demonstrate perception-driven whole-body reconfiguration for autonomous
emergency assessment.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work in robotic ultrasound,

Section~\ref{sec:problem_formulation} formally defines the system and the

Section~\ref{sec:active_perception} presents the active perception framework,

Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the decoupled whole-body reconfiguration
