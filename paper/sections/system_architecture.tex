\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_architecture}

The \ac{TERESA} system is organised into four layers---hardware, perception,
mission management, and control---communicating over \ac{ROS}~2 with
\ac{DDS}. Computation is distributed across three units: the Spot onboard
computer SpotCore (base control and odometry), a NVIDIA Jetson (perception
and arm control), and an external PC (mission coordination and \ac{WBC}).
All components described in this section are available as open-source
software.

\subsection{Hardware Platform}

The \textbf{Boston Dynamics Spot} quadruped provides mobile navigation on
unstructured terrain. It is commanded via its onboard \ac{ROS}~2 driver
(\texttt{spot\_ros2}) through velocity commands $[v_x, \omega_z]$ on the
\texttt{/cmd\_vel} topic and body posture commands on the
\texttt{/body\_pose} topic. The \textbf{Unitree Z1} is a six-\ac{DOF}
serial manipulator with a payload of \SI{2}{\kilogram}, mounted rigidly on
Spot's back. It is controlled at the torque level through a \ac{JTC}
interface running on the Jetson. The \textbf{ultrasound probe} is attached
to the Z1 flange ($\mathcal{F}_{EE}$).

Two depth cameras provide complementary coverage. A 360-degree camera
would offer panoramic awareness but lacks depth sensing, which is essential
for projecting skeleton keypoints to 3D world coordinates. Spot's five
built-in stereo cameras are grayscale and positioned low on the body for
ground-plane obstacle avoidance; they are not suitable for RGB-based
\ac{YOLO} skeleton detection nor for viewing a patient's torso. Instead,
\ac{TERESA} uses two \ac{RGBD} sensors in complementary roles. An
\textbf{Orbbec Femto Bolt \ac{RGBD}} camera is mounted on Spot's body,
providing wide-field colour and depth for patient detection at distances up
to \SI{3}{\metre}. An \textbf{Intel RealSense D435 \ac{RGBD}} camera is
mounted at the Z1 \ac{EE}; the arm's \ac{WBC} look-at controller positions it
as an active pan-tilt unit, providing close-range depth and colour for
surface estimation and torso tracking during approach and scanning.

The \ac{TF} tree defines the spatial relationships between all system
components. Fig.~\ref{fig:frame_tree} illustrates the frame hierarchy:

           $\to$ \texttt{my\_spot/body} branches into the Orbbec chain

           shown as \texttt{orbbec\_color}) and the Z1 arm chain
           (\texttt{world} $\to$ \texttt{link00} $\to$ $\cdots$

           $\to$ \texttt{camera\_color\_optical\_frame},
           shown as \texttt{camera\_color}).}
  \label{fig:frame_tree}
\end{figure}

The key properties of this tree are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{my\_spot/odom} is world-fixed, published by SpotCore
        odometry.
  \item \texttt{my\_spot/body} moves with the base (height, pitch, and yaw
        are set via \texttt{/body\_pose}).
  \item \texttt{body $\to$ orbbec\_link} and \texttt{body $\to$ world} are
        static transforms representing the calibrated Orbbec and Z1 mount
        positions.
  \item \texttt{world $\to$ link00} is a fixed joint in the Z1 URDF,
        published by \texttt{robot\_state\_publisher}. The Z1 arm chain
        \texttt{link00 $\to$ $\cdots$ $\to$ link06} follows.
  \item \texttt{link06 $\to$ camera\_link $\to$ camera\_color\_optical\_frame}
        defines the RealSense pose on the \ac{EE}.
\end{itemize}
Because the \texttt{body $\to$ world} transform is static, the entire Z1
kinematic tree moves rigidly with Spot, and any change in body posture
automatically propagates to the arm base frame $\mathcal{F}_0$.

\subsection{Perception Pipeline}

\subsubsection{Wide-field patient detection (Orbbec)}

During the SEARCHING and approach phases, \ac{YOLO}11-pose~\cite{maji2022}
runs on the Orbbec \ac{RGBD} stream and detects the patient's skeleton. A
posture classifier labels the patient's pose (standing, sitting, lying) and
outputs a confidence score $c \in [0, 1]$. A laying-human detector
computes the approach point as the torso centroid of the four \ac{COCO}
keypoints (shoulders and hips), back-projected to 3D via the calibrated
depth map. It also publishes the torso centroid position---the
body\_center---as a separate \texttt{PoseStamped} message, used by the
\ac{WBC} look-at controller to orient the \ac{EE} camera toward the
patient's body during the pre-approach phase. The approach point is
expressed in the camera optical frame; the \ac{TF} tree transforms it to
$\mathcal{F}_W$ for target locking
(Section~\ref{sec:confidence_search}).

\subsubsection{Close-range torso tracking (RealSense)}

Once the mission transitions to the approach sequence, the \ac{EE}-mounted
RealSense camera becomes active. A \ac{YOLO}-based torso tracker with a
six-state \ac{KF} (position and velocity) publishes a filtered torso
estimate $\hat{\mathbf{p}}(t)$ and a tracker state. Three tracking states
are relevant during the approach: ESTIMATING (the Kalman filter is
converging on at least three torso keypoints with sufficient confidence),
LOCKED (the estimate has stabilised with low spatial variance), and GUIDING
(a lenient mode used during active search where any partial skeleton
detection may trigger the semi-locking phase). A surface estimation node
fits a plane to the depth point cloud in a region of interest around the
detected torso, publishing the surface centroid $\mathbf{p}_s$ and outward
normal $\mathbf{n}_s$ as a \texttt{PoseStamped} message.

\subsubsection{Sensor handoff strategy}

The transition from Orbbec to RealSense is not a hard switch but a
confidence-driven handoff. During PRE\_APPROACH the coordinator requires
several consecutive readings from the RealSense tracker in either the
ESTIMATING or LOCKED state before advancing to the approach phase. This
ensures that the close-range sensor has reliably acquired the patient
before the Orbbec loses the target at short range. If the RealSense fails
to confirm within a timeout, the system still proceeds, falling back to
the odometry target acquired during the locking phase.

\texttt{wbc\_coordinator} node. Fig.~\ref{fig:fsm} shows the state

active in each state.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Waiting TF}. The system waits for the \texttt{tf\_monitor}
        node to confirm that all \ac{TF} chains and hardware topics are
        available. This gate prevents premature activation when SpotCore
        \ac{DDS} discovery is still in progress.
  \item \textbf{Idle}. All \ac{TF} ready. The system waits for a start
        command from the keyboard operator.
  \item \textbf{Searching}. Spot sweeps through a sequence of body yaw
        orientations in a coarse search, while the arm executes an
        ACTIVE\_SEARCH mode with wide Cartesian poses. Two detection
        mechanisms run in parallel: Orbbec direct detection of a lying
        patient and RealSense guidance mode for partial body detection
        (Section~\ref{sec:confidence_search}).
  \item \textbf{Semi-locking}. The RealSense has detected a potential
        body but Orbbec confirmation is pending. Spot rotates toward the
        detected torso direction while the arm maintains a \ac{WBC} look-at
        orientation. The Orbbec receives a clean observation window to
        confirm the detection. Upon confirmation, the system transitions to
        locking; on timeout, it resumes searching from the current
        orientation.
  \item \textbf{Locking}. The Orbbec has confirmed a lying patient. The arm
        returns to an elevated home pose, while the coordinator collects
        a sequence of approach point samples. The transition to
        pre-approach is gated by both the \ac{IK} trajectory completion
        and the collection of the required number of samples.
  \item \textbf{Pre-approach}. Spot straightens to an upright posture.
        The \ac{WBC} arm look-at controller orients the RealSense toward
        the patient's torso centroid (body\_center, computed from the Orbbec
        skeleton during locking). The coordinator waits for consecutive
        ESTIMATING or LOCKED tracker readings before authorising the
        approach. A global timeout ensures the mission progresses even if
        the tracker cannot achieve full lock.
  \item \textbf{Approaching}. The Spot navigator drives toward the approach
        target while the adaptive anticipatory body scanner
        (Section~\ref{sec:anticipatory_scan}) executes the Cartesian scan
        grid concurrently with base motion. A global timeout guards against
        unreachable targets, aborting to idle if Spot fails to reach the
        handoff distance.
  \item \textbf{Scanning}. Handoff distance reached. Spot assumes a lowered
        body posture. The body scanner publishes the FAST target set. The
        coordinator executes the pre-planned body reconfiguration between
        targets (Section~\ref{sec:method}), while the Z1 \ac{FSM} drives
        the arm through each scanning target.
  \item \textbf{WS ext}. If a target is reported unreachable, Spot
        executes a micro-step to bring the target back into the workspace.
        Once confirmed, the \ac{FSM} returns to scanning.
\end{itemize}

At any time, a stop command from the keyboard immediately transitions the
\ac{FSM} to idle, bringing the base to a halt and returning the arm to a
safe configuration.

\subsection{Z1 FSM Integration with WBC Coordinator}

The Z1 \ac{FSM} and the WBC coordinator communicate through a small set
of \ac{ROS} topics to synchronise their state machines.

In standalone mode (no WBC), the Z1 \ac{FSM} executes its own body
scanning phase after homing. When integrated with \ac{TERESA}, the
\ac{FSM} enters WAITING after homing and listens for two conditions:
\texttt{/wbc/state = `SCANNING'} (indicating that the approach is
complete) and \texttt{/z1/fast\_ready = True} (indicating that the
anticipatory body scanner has computed the FAST target set). If both are
received, the \ac{FSM} skips its internal \texttt{BODY\_SCANNING} phase
entirely, avoiding redundant scanning.

During the FAST cycle, the WBC coordinator optimises Spot's body posture
for each target and broadcasts \texttt{/wbc/body\_ready}. The Z1
\ac{FSM} waits for this signal before executing the approach to the next
target. After each target is reached, the \ac{FSM} publishes
\texttt{/z1/next\_point\_idx} to request the next body posture
optimisation. This synchronisation loop continues for all $N$ targets.

\subsection{Operational Flow}

The full mission is launched from five terminal sessions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{teresa\_core.launch.py} --- Spot driver, Orbbec driver,
        RealSense driver, Z1 bringup, static \ac{TF} transforms,
        \texttt{tf\_monitor}.
  \item \texttt{teresa\_perception.launch.py} --- Orbbec YOLO perception
        nodes and RealSense YOLO torso tracker.
  \item \texttt{z1\_control.launch.py} --- Z1 arm control stack
        (\ac{IK}, \ac{FSM}, controller switching, impedance).
  \item \texttt{wbc.launch.py} --- WBC coordinator, \ac{QP} controller,
        spot navigator, \ac{IK} goal multiplexer.
  \item \texttt{wbc\_keyboard\_node} --- Operator keyboard interface
        (start, stop, emergency return).
\end{enumerate}

Upon launch, the \texttt{tf\_monitor} verifies all \ac{TF} chains and
hardware topics. When ready, the keyboard operator presses the start
key, and the mission proceeds through the \ac{FSM} states automatically
until all scanning targets are completed or an emergency stop is
triggered.
