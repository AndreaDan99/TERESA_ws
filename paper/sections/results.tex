\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Search Time Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:search_time} presents the search time results from the Monte
Carlo simulation. Across all \num{10000} random patient orientations, the
Spot+Arm configuration achieves a mean search time of \SI{8.2}{\second},
compared to \SI{18.3}{\second} for the Orbbec-Only configuration, yielding a
\textbf{2.2}$\times$ mean speedup. The median speedup is even more
pronounced at \textbf{3.4}$\times$ (\SI{5.7}{\second} vs.\
\SI{19.4}{\second}), indicating that for the majority of patient orientations
the arm provides a substantial advantage. The 95th percentile times are
closer (\SI{32.6}{\second} vs.\ \SI{36.9}{\second}, \textbf{1.1}$\times$),
reflecting the worst-case scenario where the patient is oriented opposite to
the robot's initial heading and both configurations must sweep through nearly
the full azimuth.

At individual yaw angles, the speedup ranges from \textbf{3.4}$\times$ at
$45\degree$ (\SI{3.1}{\second} vs.\ \SI{10.7}{\second}) to \textbf{1.0}$\times$
at $180\degree$, where both configurations require a full base rotation to
the opposite side. At $0\degree$ (patient facing the robot), the arm provides
a \textbf{3.3}$\times$ speedup (\SI{0.6}{\second} vs.\ \SI{2.0}{\second}),
as the \ac{EE}-mounted camera can immediately detect the patient without
waiting for the base to rotate. At $90\degree$ and $135\degree$, the speedup
is \textbf{1.9}$\times$ and \textbf{2.4}$\times$ respectively, demonstrating
consistent advantage across the frontal hemisphere.

The arm's benefit is most significant at conservative base yaw speeds. The
simulation uses \SI{0.2}{\radian\per\second}, matching the real system's
safe operating limit. At a faster yaw speed of \SI{0.5}{\radian\per\second},
the mean speedup drops to \textbf{1.4}$\times$, as the base rotation time
becomes less dominant in the total search budget. This confirms that the
arm's angular coverage is especially valuable when the base must move slowly
for safety or stability reasons, which is the typical operating regime for
medical response robots in close proximity to a patient.

Overall, the \textbf{2.2}$\times$ mean speedup means that \ac{TERESA}
effectively halves the expected time to localise a patient at an unknown
orientation, reducing the critical first-response delay from approximately
\SI{18}{\second} to \SI{8}{\second}. This reduction is achieved without any
additional sensing hardware: the Z1 arm, already required for ultrasound
probe manipulation, serves a dual role as an active perception platform
during the search phase.
