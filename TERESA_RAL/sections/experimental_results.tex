\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experimental_results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Experiments were conducted on the physical Spot+Z1 platform integrating a
Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped, a Unitree Z1 six-\ac{DOF} arm, an Orbbec Femto
Bolt \ac{RGBD} camera on Spot's body, and an Intel RealSense D435 \ac{RGBD}
camera at the Z1 \ac{EE}. Computation was distributed across SpotCore (base
control), a NVIDIA Jetson (perception and arm control), and an external PC
(mission coordination and \ac{WBC}), communicating over \ac{ROS}~2 Jazzy.

The experimental scenario simulated a mass-casualty incident with a supine
patient placed at an unknown location on flat terrain. The patient remained
stationary throughout each trial. The robot started from a fixed initial pose
and was required to autonomously search for, localise, approach, and scan the
patient's abdomen. Ten patient positions were defined, varying distance
(\SI{2}{\metre}--\SI{4}{\metre}) and angular offset ($-45^\circ$ to
$+45^\circ$) relative to the robot's initial heading. Each position was tested
with both operational modes, yielding twenty paired trials.

Two operational modes were compared:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Baseline}: sequential stop-and-manipulate. The robot searches
        for the patient, navigates to the approach point, stops, performs a
        fixed four-pose body scan, and then executes the \ac{FAST} scanning
        sequence. The body scan does not overlap with navigation.
  \item \textbf{\ac{TERESA}}: the proposed active perception pipeline, in which
        the adaptive anticipatory scanner runs concurrently with base
        navigation, the scan grid adapts to pre-existing torso keypoint
        confidence, and the QualityMonitor modulates approach velocity.
\end{itemize}

Five metrics were recorded for each trial: (i)~total mission time from search
initiation to completion of the final \ac{FAST} target; (ii)~idle time, defined
as periods during which neither the base nor the arm executes a productive
task; (iii)~scan overlap, the fraction of the body scan completed before the
base reaches the handoff distance; (iv)~probe positioning error, the Euclidean
distance between the achieved \ac{EE} position and the desired target pose at
each \ac{FAST} point; and (v)~lock time from search initiation to patient
localisation confirmation.

\subsection{Search Phase: Whole-Body Coverage and Dual-Sensor Lock}

We isolated the arm's contribution to search through an ablation experiment:
\ac{TERESA} (full system) versus a Spot-only baseline with the Z1 arm disabled
and only the front-facing Orbbec active. Twenty trials were conducted at five
body yaw angles ($0^\circ$--$180^\circ$, two repetitions each), measuring
time to Orbbec posture confidence $\geq$ \num{0.70}.

With the arm's seven scanning poses providing full \SI{360}{\degree} coverage
from a single base position, \ac{TERESA} required no base rotation: frontal
patients were detected near-instantly, the rest during the scan, yielding a
mean of $\sim$\SI{3}{\second}. Without the arm, the fixed camera's
\SI{77}{\degree} effective field of view forced five discrete base rotations
($\sim$\SI{7}{\second} each at \SI{0.2}{\radian\per\second}, plus
\SI{2.0}{\second} dwell), giving $\sim$\SI{15}{\second}---a
$\sim$\textbf{5}$\times$ slowdown. A \num{10000}-trial Monte Carlo simulation
confirmed this ratio across all patient orientations.

The dual-sensor architecture added robustness independently of speed: in three
of the ten \ac{TERESA} trials, the Orbbec alone failed to lock. These failures
stemmed from the Orbbec's fixed horizontal viewpoint at
\SI{0.5}{\metre} above the ground, which is suboptimal for detecting a supine
body whose visual features---torso, limbs, head---are primarily visible from
above. The arm-mounted RealSense, positioned by the \ac{WBC} look-at controller
to observe the scene from an elevated angle, detected partial torso keypoints
in these cases and guided the base toward the body, giving the Orbbec a second
observation window at a more favourable orientation. Without this redundancy,
these trials would have resumed the coarse search cycle, adding
$\sim$\SI{14}{\second}. The arm thus serves two complementary roles: its
\SI{360}{\degree} coverage eliminates the base-rotation bottleneck, while its
elevated viewpoint provides the sensor redundancy that prevents detection
failures a single-camera system would incur.

\subsection{End-to-End Mission Performance}

Across all twenty end-to-end trials, \ac{TERESA} reduced total mission time
from $\sim$\SI{100}{\second} to $\sim$\SI{90}{\second} ($\sim$\SI{10}{\percent}).
The gain was concentrated in idle time: in the baseline, the body scan executed
after the base stopped, adding $\sim$\SI{10}{\second} of idle time; \ac{TERESA}
absorbed this into the approach, completing $\sim$\SI{80}{\percent} of the scan
before reaching the handoff point. The adaptive scan grid accelerated this
further: in the majority of trials, torso keypoints were already resolved before
approach, and the grid automatically selected the minimal two-pose pattern
rather than the full four-pose sweep. Pre-planned body reconfiguration reduced
probe positioning error from $\sim$\SI{45}{\milli\metre} to
$\sim$\SI{30}{\milli\metre}, with the largest improvement at lateral
\ac{FAST} targets. The exposure scan dominated the mission budget at
$\sim$\SI{40}{\second} in both configurations, limiting the overall speedup.
In manual operation, operators could skip unreachable grid points, reducing
total time to $\sim$\SI{70}{\second} at the cost of incomplete coverage.

\subsection{Whole-Body Reconfiguration}

The body posture optimizer described in Section~\ref{sec:body_reconf} proved
essential for reaching distant anatomical targets on a full-scale body. We
compared two configurations: a fixed nominal posture (height \SI{0}{\metre},
pitch \SI{0}{\degree}, no base displacement) and the optimised posture produced
by the grid search over body height $h$, pitch $\varphi$, and longitudinal base
displacement $dy$.  The cost function included a regularisation term
$\lambda\,|dy|$ (with $\lambda = 0.50$) that penalised walking, encouraging
the optimiser to prefer height and pitch adjustments---which are faster and
consume less energy---over base displacement.  The difference was not marginal:
without optimisation, only $\sim$\SI{40}{\percent} of exposure grid points and
$\sim$\SI{50}{\percent} of \ac{FAST} targets fell within the Z1 arm's
reachable workspace.  With the optimiser's \ac{IK}-driven retry loop
escalating from 2D~($h, \varphi$) to 3D~($dy, h, \varphi$) when the initial
posture failed, completion rose to $\sim$\SI{85}{\percent} and
\SI{100}{\percent} respectively.  Points that timed out at both tiers were
skipped---the arm cannot reach them even with full base repositioning.  The
residual \SI{15}{\percent} of exposure points were at the furthest extremities
(head, feet), beyond the arm's maximum reach.  In the fixed-posture baseline,
these unreachable points were simply skipped; with reconfiguration, the
mission achieved near-complete coverage without operator intervention.

\subsection{Discussion}

The results demonstrate that treating body posture as an active perceptual
resource yields measurable improvements in mission efficiency. The concurrent
scanning approach eliminates a structural source of idle time inherent in
sequential architectures: the body scan, which in the baseline adds a full scan
duration to the critical path after navigation, is absorbed into the approach
phase. The adaptive grid further accelerates the scan when perception quality
is already high, making the system efficient in the favourable case without
sacrificing coverage when keypoints are unresolved.

The dual-sensor architecture addresses a fundamental limitation of single-view
patient detection. The wide-field Orbbec provides reliable posture
classification when the patient is well-centred in the frame, but its fixed
mounting on Spot's body limits its effective field of regard to the robot's
current orientation. The \ac{EE}-mounted RealSense, actively positioned by the
\ac{WBC} look-at controller, compensates for this limitation by guiding the
base to orientations where the Orbbec can succeed. This complementary coverage
is particularly valuable in emergency scenarios where patient position and
lighting conditions cannot be controlled.

Several limitations warrant discussion. The current system assumes a single
stationary patient on flat terrain. Multi-patient scenarios and non-flat
terrain remain outside the scope of this work. The impedance controller
provides compliant probe contact, but the experiments were conducted with the
probe positioned at the skin surface without clinical scanning pressure;
ultrasound image quality assessment was not performed. The injury detection
pipeline relies on pre-trained models whose performance may degrade on wound
types or skin tones underrepresented in the training data. These aspects are
the subject of ongoing work.
