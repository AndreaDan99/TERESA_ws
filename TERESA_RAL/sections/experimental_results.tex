\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experimental_results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\draft{Experiments ran on the Spotzi platform} with an Orbbec Femto
Bolt on Spot's body and a RealSense D415 at the \ac{EE}.

\draft{All neural network inference runs on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX
(\ac{ROS}~2 Humble, CUDA 11.4, PyTorch 2.0): YOLO11n-pose at 23--30\,fps,
NLF at 13--17\,fps, Grounding DINO at 1--2\,fps. Spot's integrated computer
handles base control and odometry.}

Ten supine mannequin
positions were defined (\SI{2}{\metre}--\SI{4}{\metre},
$\pm\SI{45}{\degree}$); each was tested in two configurations, yielding
twenty paired end-to-end trials. \draft{We also conducted a separate search ablation
with twenty additional trials at five orientations
($0^\circ$--$180^\circ$), comparing the full \ac{TERESA} system against a
Spot-only baseline with the arm disabled.}

\draft{The two end-to-end configurations differ in three aspects. \textbf{Baseline}
uses a conventional sequential pipeline: navigation, torso mapping, and
scanning execute in strict order with no concurrency; body posture is fixed
at Spot's nominal stance; the exposure scan uses a fixed three-pose pattern;
body reconfiguration is disabled. \textbf{\ac{TERESA}} adds anticipatory
torso mapping concurrent with approach (QualityMonitor,
Section~\ref{sec:phase_approaching}), an adaptive exposure grid (two or three
poses depending on keypoint confidence), and pre-planned two-tier body
reconfiguration (Section~\ref{sec:body_reconf}) for every \ac{FAST} target.}

\draft{We evaluate five metrics:
\emph{total completion time} $T_{\text{total}}$ (wall-clock from start to
Idle);
\emph{lock time} $T_{\text{lock}}$ (time to confirmed patient lock);
\emph{scan overlap} $\eta = N_{\text{pre}} / N_{\text{total}}$ (fraction of
torso model completed during approach; a value near~1 means mapping imposed
no extra time);
\emph{idle time} $T_{\text{idle}}$ (cumulative stationary time); and
\emph{\ac{EE} positioning error}
$e_i = \|\mathbf{p}_{EE,i} - \mathbf{p}_i^*\|$ (Euclidean distance to each
\ac{FAST} target, reported as mean and standard deviation across all
kinematically reachable targets).}

\subsection{End-to-End System Performance}
\draft{Across all twenty end-to-end trials, \ac{TERESA} reduced total
completion time by \SI{10.5}{\percent} (Table~\ref{tab:endtoend}).} The gain
came from eliminating idle time---the anticipatory torso mapper completed
$\sim$\SI{80}{\percent} of the torso mapping concurrently with approach---and from the adaptive grid, which selected the minimal two-pose pattern in the
majority of trials.  Note that the baseline benefits from an optimistic
assumption: without body reconfiguration, many \ac{FAST} targets are
unreachable from the default posture
(Section~\ref{sec:body_reconf_results}).  Manual operation, where
operators skipped unreachable grid points, reduced total time to
$\sim$\SI{70}{\second} at the cost of incomplete coverage.  The principal
contribution of \ac{TERESA} is therefore not speed but capability: without
whole-body reconfiguration, the baseline simply cannot reach many anatomical
targets and returns incomplete scans with higher positioning error.
\ac{TERESA} achieves systematic, precise, and complete assessment across all
targets, which the baseline structurally cannot deliver.

\subsection{Search Phase: Whole-Body Coverage and Dual-Sensor Lock}
\ac{TERESA} required no base rotation: the arm's seven scanning poses
provided full \SI{360}{\degree} coverage as the base walked forward,
yielding a mean lock time of \SI{3.2 \pm 1.1}{\second}. The Spot-only
baseline, limited to the Orbbec's \SI{77}{\degree} field of view, needed five
discrete base rotations ($\sim$\SI{7}{\second} each at
\SI{0.2}{\radian\per\second}, plus \SI{2.0}{\second} dwell), taking
\SI{14.8 \pm 2.3}{\second}---a \textbf{5.0}$\times$ slowdown confirmed by a
\num{10000}-trial Monte Carlo simulation.

The dual-sensor architecture added robustness independently of speed: in
five \ac{TERESA} trials the Orbbec alone failed to lock because its fixed
horizontal viewpoint (\SI{0.5}{\metre} above ground) is suboptimal for
detecting a supine body. The arm-mounted RealSense, elevated by the
\ac{WBC} look-at controller, detected partial torso keypoints and guided the
base to a more favorable orientation for the Orbbec. Without this
redundancy, these trials would have resumed the search cycle, adding
$\sim$\SI{14}{\second}. The arm thus serves two complementary roles:
\SI{360}{\degree} coverage eliminates the base-rotation bottleneck, while
its elevated viewpoint provides sensor redundancy against single-camera
detection failures.

\subsection{Whole-Body Reconfiguration}
\label{sec:body_reconf_results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:posture_results} compares four posture configurations of
increasing capability. The fixed nominal posture (Spot's default stance)
reaches only a fraction of targets. A single heuristic posture
(\SI{-0.15}{\metre} height, \SI{5}{\degree} pitch) improves coverage but
cannot adapt to varying target positions. The \draft{in-place}
optimiser selects the best $(h, \varphi)$ per target via
kinematically-aware stance search with \ac{IK}-driven retry. The
\draft{base-displacement} optimiser adds longitudinal base walking
$dy$ with the regularised cost
$J = \|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{sweet}}\| + \lambda\,|dy|$
($\lambda = 0.50$): the penalty encourages height and pitch adjustments over
walking, while still allowing displacement when necessary. The residual
\SI{15}{\percent} of exposure points at the furthest extremities (head,
feet) were beyond the arm's maximum reach even with full base repositioning and were skipped gracefully.
